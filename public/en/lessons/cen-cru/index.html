Centaurus, Crux, Musca — how to find them in the sky

Centaurus · Centaur, gen. Centauri · Cen
Crux · Southern Cross, gen. Crucis · Cru
Musca · Fly, gen. Muscae · Mus

Open the lesson on the interactive map

How to find it

This part of the sky is no longer visible from mid-northern latitudes. Move south from Virgo and Hydra: below them is Centaurus. Its two brightest stars, α (Rigil Kentaurus) and β (Hadar), are the “pointers” to the Southern Cross (Crux). Extend the long bar of the Cross (Gacrux → Acrux) 4.5 times — you will reach the south celestial pole. Below the Cross is the small Musca, the Fly.

What it looks like

The Cross is a small “kite” of four bright stars. Centaurus is large and wraps around the Cross on three sides.

Good to know

  • α Centauri is the nearest star system (4.3 light-years). Its third star, Proxima, is the nearest star to the Sun (4.24 light-years).
  • The Southern Cross is the smallest constellation in the sky (88th by area). It is shown on the flags of Australia, New Zealand and Brazil.
  • There is no bright star at the south celestial pole: the nearest noticeable one is σ Octantis (5.4m).
  • ω Centauri is the brightest globular cluster in the sky. Next to the Cross is the dark Coalsack nebula.
  • Rigil Kentaurus (δ ≈ −61°) never rises north of latitude 29° N.
