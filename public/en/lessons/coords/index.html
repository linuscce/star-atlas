Coordinates and visibility

Open the lesson on the interactive map

Most olympiad problems about the starry sky are solved with three formulas and knowing where the bright stars are.

Is a star visible at latitude φ

  • A star never sets if δ > 90° − φ.
  • A star never rises if δ < −(90° − φ).
  • Altitude at upper culmination (in the south): h = 90° − φ + δ.

Example: φ ≈ 56° (Moscow, Edinburgh). Never set: stars with δ > 34° (Vega, Capella, Deneb, the Dipper). Never rise: stars with δ < −34° (Canopus, Achernar, the Southern Cross). Sirius (δ = −17°) rises to 90 − 56 − 17 = 17°.

Which stars are in the south at midnight

The right ascension of the Sun on March 21 is ≈ 0h and grows by about 2h per month. At midnight the stars in the south have α ≈ αSun + 12h.

Example: on January 1 the Sun has α ≈ 18h40m, so at midnight α ≈ 6h40m is in the south — that is Sirius (6h45m). On winter nights Orion is visible, on summer nights Scorpius.

Sidereal time

A star culminates when the local sidereal time equals its right ascension. A sidereal day is 3 min 56 s shorter than a solar day, so every evening the stars rise ~4 minutes earlier, and in a month the picture of the sky shifts by ~2 hours.

Precession

The Earth’s axis traces a cone over ~26,000 years. That is why the celestial pole (Thuban → Polaris → Vega), the equinox points (the vernal one moved from Aries to Pisces) and the dates when the Sun is in each constellation slowly shift.
