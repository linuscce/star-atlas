How to read a star map

Open the lesson on the interactive map

This is a real map of the sky: the 5,000 stars visible to the naked eye. You can drag it (with the mouse or a finger) and zoom in (with the mouse wheel, two fingers or the +/− buttons).

North is up, east is on the left

On maps of the Earth east is on the right, but on star maps it is on the left. Why? A map of the Earth is a view from above, while we look at the sky from below, from the inside. Lie on your back with your head to the north, and east will be on your left. All olympiad star maps are drawn this way.

The size of a dot is brightness

The brightness of stars is measured in magnitudes (m). The smaller the number, the brighter the star: Sirius is −1.5m, Polaris 2m, and the faintest stars visible to the naked eye in a dark place are about 6m. A difference of 5m is exactly a factor of 100 in brightness.

How stars are named

Bright stars have proper names (Vega, Rigel), but in problems they are more often written in Bayer form: a Greek letter plus the Latin genitive of the constellation — α Lyrae = Vega, β Orionis = Rigel. Usually α is the brightest star of the constellation, β the second and so on, but there are exceptions. The Greek alphabet is in the “Reference” tab — learn it first.

Coordinates

The grid on the map shows equatorial coordinates: declination δ (from −90° to +90°, like latitude) and right ascension α (from 0h to 24h, increasing to the east — to the left). The bright line is the celestial equator (δ = 0°).

How the lessons work

Each lesson is one constellation (or several small ones). The new constellation is drawn in blue, the ones already learned in grey, and the yellow arrows show how to find the new one from stars you already know. This way the sky becomes a single network that starts from the Big Dipper.

At the end of a lesson, press “Test yourself”: the lines and labels disappear, and you have to find the stars yourself. Every few lessons, visit “Practice” — it asks about all the constellations you have learned.
